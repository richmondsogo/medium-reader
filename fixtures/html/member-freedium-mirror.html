The Most Pleasant People to Be Around Have This in Common

It’s not confidence, charm, or charisma

By Nadia Tidona

4 min read

You are at a gathering, and a family member touches you when you're clearly not open to physical contact.

You're sitting quietly during a party, and someone walks up and comes to poke you to get you out of your shell.

You are in a group of people and have no desire to partake in the conversation, but someone asks you the most polarizing question and now all eyes are on you.

I have countless examples of this, and I suspect you have too:

People cannot read the signals.

Not when they are clearly, verbally expressed, and even less when they're implicit.

The way most people are disconnected from themselves can leak out and affect the people around them, too.

A recent interaction I had made me think about this, although this time it was a positive example of what happens when someone does have that awareness.

I was at a baby shower. I hadn't seen some of the participants since university. A lot can change in fifteen years, and it's nice to catch up on what's happening in each other's lives.

A friend and I started chatting with one of the girls I hadn't seen in ages, and the conversation casually landed on a topic she clearly was uncomfortable with.

"Do you know what her job is?" my friend asked. "No." I asked the girl, "What's your job?"

"Medical examiner", she said.

Clearly, she didn't want to get on the topic, so we naturally and easily moved the conversation somewhere else.

Nothing dramatic happened, no boundary had to be stated, no one had to explain themselves, no excuses or apologies had to be given.

We simply noticed.

In another situation, I would have watched something very different happen: a well-meaning person who wasn't reading the signals bombarding a clearly unsettled person with questions they had no intention of answering, but felt pressured to answer anyway.

We've all been there.

Not everyone has the self-awareness to say "I don't want to talk about it", or "this conversation is making me uncomfortable". And often, before we even realize it, we've entered a conversation that will make us feel uneasy for days to come.

Even the most well-meaning person can lack the attunement to read the vibes of the room and assess implicit boundaries before doing something that can make someone uncomfortable.

And this is annoying.

There's a whole movement about speaking up and taking space, and I'm all about it, but attunement to the person your authentic expression will impact is just as important as connection and loyalty to yourself.

Otherwise, sorry, but you're just being an asshole.

You don't get to enter someone's space and not acknowledge their implicit boundaries before involving them in something they might not be open to.

I know a lot of people don't realize it, but this shouldn't be an excuse.

We all have the same senses and access to a multitude of resources to train our relational intelligence. Anyone who cares about being considerate to others and a positive presence in people's lives can learn the art of relational awareness.

Because the point is: your freedom exists in relationship with other people's freedom too, and you cannot ignore that.

I call this ability to tune into the vibes of the people around you and read the non-verbal cues effectively "relational awareness".

The pattern I've noticed about people who tend to lack it is that they are usually disconnected from their own signals, too.

It makes sense, if you think about it:

How can you be attuned to what's going on for someone else and show empathy for them when you are used to overriding your emotions, distracting yourself to feel better, and performing the most socially acceptable version of yourself in public?

You can't give others what you don't give yourself.

And treating yourself with empathy, tact, and attunement can be difficult if no one has done that for you growing up.

It's still common practice today to belittle the big emotions of children, because they are uncomfortable or embarrassing for their parents.

"It's nothing," many parents might tell a kid who just fell and got scared, hoping to make him stop crying.

What if "do you need a hug, or do you want to go sit there with me for a moment?" was instead a better option that would allow that kid to be more attuned to what's going on for others as an adult?

What if taking time to be with what you're experiencing is the exact thing that would allow you to be more attuned to what's going on for others?

If you've done some reparenting or inner work, you know it:

Noticing things that you previously would have dismissed or that weren't a concern of yours is one of the first signs of a nervous system that's finally out of chronic survival.

When you're not constantly worried about what might happen or busy dwelling on what previously went wrong, you have more resources available to higher functions — like attunement to others, for example.

We are social animals. No matter how much of an introvert or extrovert one might be, none of us is designed to function individually and in isolation.

Attunement to others' inner landscape is something we're wired for. But it requires presence, safety, and practice. It requires that we get out of our heads and ask:

  • Can I sense others' state without making it about me?
  • Can I read the room without trying to prove something or perform?
  • Can I speak my truth while being aware of the person receiving it?

I've heard someone's TEDx talk once about how most women worry about whether or not the person in front of them will notice their imperfection, instead of letting themselves be fully immersed in the interaction.

I'm not sure that's true for most women, but that definitely does happen.

And it's a shame, because if we keep our focus on ourselves, we can never be truly present with the other person and the interaction will always be limited.

But when you make it a point to get out of your head and fully be in relation to the other person, you will find yourself being a better speaker, a better listener, someone able to attract more opportunities, and someone others genuinely enjoy having around.

I've spent most of my life thinking I always had to say something smart or funny whenever I was in a group.

Healing that fear of being irrelevant and allowing myself to just be in silence opened up a whole new way of relating that is infinitely less stressful and more meaningful for me and everyone else involved.

So believe me when I say that the ability to stay connected to yourself while sensing what is happening around you is a skill worth cultivating.

Not only is it good for you, but it also makes you a safe person to be around.

And God knows, we need more of those people.
